Project 5: Diffusion Models

Part 0: Setup and Sampling

Overview

In this section, I experimented with the DeepFloyd IF diffusion model to generate images from text prompts. I created custom text prompts and generated their embeddings using the T5 Encoder. The model was tested with different numbers of inference steps to observe the quality variations.

Random Seed

Throughout this project, I used random seed: 100 for reproducibility.

Custom Text Prompts and Generated Images

I tested three prompts with varying levels of detail to understand how prompt specificity affects generation quality. The prompts used were (from left to right):

  • "a photo of a dog"
  • "a DSLR photo of a dog, high quality"
  • "A highly detailed, photorealistic portrait photo of a medium sized dog sitting calmly outdoors. The dog has soft, well groomed fur with visible individual hair strands, natural color variations, and realistic texture. The dog is facing the camera with expressive eyes, sharp focus on the face, and a relaxed, friendly expression. Shot with a professional DSLR camera, shallow depth of field, natural lighting, soft sunlight illuminating the fur. Background is softly blurred with bokeh effect, showing a green park environment. Ultra high resolution, realistic proportions, accurate anatomy, natural shadows, cinematic composition, no distortion."

More detailed prompts with specific constraints and quality descriptors (e.g., "high quality", "photorealistic", "DSLR") produce higher quality images with better adherence to the desired attributes. Additionally, using more inference steps tends to generate images with finer details and higher overall quality.

Part 1: Diffusion Model Sampling

1.1 Implementing the Forward Process

The forward process adds noise to a clean image according to the diffusion equation. I implemented the forward(im, t) function that adds noise at timestep t using the formula:

$$x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \epsilon, \quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$

Below shows the Campanile test image at different noise levels: t = [250, 500, 750].

Campanile with noise at t=250, t=500, and t=750

1.2 Classical Denoising

To establish a baseline, I applied classical Gaussian blur denoising to the noisy images. This approach uses a simple low-pass filter to reduce noise but cannot recover fine details.

Top: Original noisy images at t=250, 500, 750. Bottom: Gaussian blur denoised results

1.3 One-Step Denoising

Using the pretrained UNet, I estimated the noise in each noisy image and removed it in a single step. The UNet predicts the noise ε, which is then subtracted from the noisy image to recover a clean estimate. This one-step approach provides better results than Gaussian blur, especially at lower noise levels.

One-step denoising results showing original, noisy, and denoised images at t=250, 500, 750

1.4 Iterative Denoising

Instead of denoising in one step, I implemented iterative denoising that gradually removes noise over multiple steps. Starting from timestep 990 and using a stride of 30, the algorithm progressively denoises the image. This approach significantly outperforms both one-step denoising and Gaussian blur.

Comparison of iterative denoising vs one-step vs Gaussian blur. Every 5th denoising step is shown.

1.5 Diffusion Model Sampling

Starting from pure random noise, I generated completely new images using the diffusion model. Using the text prompt "a high quality photo" and applying iterative denoising from t=990 to t=0, the model creates diverse images from scratch.

Five diverse images generated from pure noise using diffusion model sampling

1.6 Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG)

Classifier-free guidance improves image quality by amplifying the conditional signal. The CFG formula combines conditional and unconditional noise estimates:

$$\epsilon = \epsilon_u + \gamma (\epsilon_c - \epsilon_u)$$

where γ is the guidance scale. Using γ=7, the generated images show stronger adherence to the text prompt.

Five images generated with Classifier-Free Guidance (γ=7)

1.7.2 Inpainting

Inpainting allows editing specific regions of an image while preserving the rest. At each denoising step, I enforce the known pixels to match the original noisy image:

$$x_t \leftarrow m \odot x_t + (1 - m) \odot \text{forward}(x_{\text{orig}}, t)$$

1.8 Visual Anagrams

Visual anagrams are images that reveal different content when viewed upside down. I implemented this by averaging the noise estimates from two different prompts, with one estimate computed on the flipped image:

$$\epsilon = (\epsilon_1 + \text{flip}(\epsilon_2)) / 2$$

Part B: Training a Diffusion Model from Scratch

Overview

In Part B, I built and trained flow matching models from scratch on the MNIST dataset. Starting with a basic single-step denoising UNet, I progressively advanced to iterative generation with time conditioning and class conditioning, demonstrating how diffusion models learn to generate data.

Part 1: Single-Step Denoising UNet

1.1 UNet Architecture

I implemented a UNet architecture with the following components:

  • Downsampling blocks using Conv2d, BatchNorm2d, and GELU activations
  • Upsampling blocks using ConvTranspose2d
  • Skip connections between encoder and decoder
  • Hidden dimension D=128

1.2 Training the Denoiser

1.2.1 Training with Fixed Noise Level (σ=0.5)

I trained the UNet to denoise MNIST digits corrupted with Gaussian noise at σ=0.5. The model was trained for 5 epochs using batch size 256 and Adam optimizer with learning rate 1e-4.

MNIST digits with different noise levels: σ = [0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.8, 1.0]

Training loss curve for single-step denoiser (σ=0.5)

Denoising results after epoch 1: Original (top), Noisy (middle), Denoised (bottom)

Denoising results after epoch 5: Original (top), Noisy (middle), Denoised (bottom)

1.2.2 Out-of-Distribution Testing

I tested the denoiser trained on σ=0.5 with various noise levels to observe its generalization. The model performs best at σ=0.5 (its training distribution) but shows degraded performance at significantly different noise levels, demonstrating the importance of training distribution.

Denoiser performance on test digits with varying noise levels [0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.8, 1.0]

1.2.3 Denoising Pure Noise

I trained a denoiser on pure Gaussian noise (σ=1.0) to explore what patterns emerge. Interestingly, the model generates blurry digit-like structures from pure noise. This happens because when trained with MSE loss on pure noise, the optimal prediction is the mean of the data distribution. Since the model cannot distinguish which specific digit to generate from pure random noise, it learns to output an average representation of all MNIST digits, resulting in these characteristic blurry images.

Part 2: Flow Matching Model

2.1-2.3 Time-Conditioned UNet

I extended the UNet to be conditioned on time by adding FCBlock operators that modulate the features based on the timestep. This allows the model to learn different denoising strategies for different noise levels in a single model.

The model was trained to predict the flow (velocity) that transforms noisy data to clean data. Training configuration:

  • Hidden dimension D=64
  • Batch size 64
  • Adam optimizer with learning rate 1e-2
  • ExponentialLR scheduler with gamma=0.1^(1.0/num_epochs)

2.4-2.6 Class-Conditioned UNet

I further extended the model to condition on digit class (0-9) using one-hot encoded vectors. This allows controlled generation of specific digits. Key features:

  • Class conditioning via additional FCBlock pairs
  • Dropout (p=0.1) to drop class information for unconditional training
  • Classifier-free guidance with γ=5.0 during sampling

The classifier-free guidance formula combines conditional and unconditional predictions:

$$\epsilon = \epsilon_{\text{uncond}} + \gamma (\epsilon_{\text{cond}} - \epsilon_{\text{uncond}})$$

Part 3: Bells & Whistles

Experiment 1: Deeper Network Architecture with Extended Training

I hypothesized that the unconditional model's poor performance was due to insufficient model expressiveness, particularly the lack of depth needed to represent the complex multimodal unconditional distribution. To test this, I added additional (3×3 convolution, BatchNorm, GELU) layers between the second DownBlock and the Flatten layer, varying the number of added layers from 2, 4, up to 128 layers.

I also extended training from 10 epochs to 100 epochs to allow the deeper model more time to converge. The results show slightly improved sampling quality in the early stages of training, but an interesting phenomenon occurs: the training loss begins to increase as training continues beyond a certain point. Despite this, the quality of generated samples does not improve significantly with the deeper architecture.

Training loss curve for deeper bottleneck architecture over 100 epochs. Note the loss increase in later epochs.

Generated samples after 20 epochs with deeper architecture

Generated samples after 100 epochs with deeper architecture

Experiment 2: Sinusoidal Timestep Embedding

The network's behavior varies significantly depending on the timestep, yet the baseline model embeds this critical information as a single scalar value. I hypothesized that this limited representation constrains the network's ability to adapt its behavior across different timesteps.

To address this, I replaced the scalar timestep embedding with sinusoidal positional encoding, which provides a richer representation of time using multiple frequency components. At the same epoch number, the model with sinusoidal embeddings generates more digit-like images compared to the scalar embedding baseline, demonstrating that a richer temporal representation helps the network learn more effectively across different noise levels.
